1. Independence: Probability and Statistics

In probability

Two events A and B are independent when knowing that one occurred does not change the probability of the other:

P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B), equivalently P(A | B) = P(A) (if P(B) > 0).

For two discrete random variables X and F, independence means that this holds for every pair of values:

P(X = x, F = f) = P(X = x)·P(F = f) for all x, f  ⇔  P(F = f | X = x) = P(F = f) for all x.

In words: the distribution of F is the same whatever the value of X.

In statistics (a sample)

From a sample of n pairs (xᵢ, fᵢ) we build the contingency table of counts nij (X = i and F = j), with row totals ni·, column totals n·j, and relative frequencies fij = nij/n. The sample is perfectly statistically independent when fij = fi··f·j in every cell, i.e. when the observed counts equal the expected frequencies under independence:

Eij = ni· · n·j / n

Equivalently, all the conditional distributions f(F = j | X = i) = nij/ni· coincide with the marginal distribution of F. Because of sampling variability, real data are almost never exactly independent even when the variables are independent in the population, so we need a test.

The χ² test

H₀: X and F are independent; H₁: they are associated. The test statistic measures the total discrepancy between observed and expected counts:

χ² = Σij (Oij − Eij)² / Eij, with df = (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom (r rows, c columns).

If H₀ is true, and the expected counts are not too small (rule of thumb: all E ≥ 5), χ² approximately follows a χ² distribution with df degrees of freedom. The p-value is the probability, under H₀, of a χ² at least as large as the one observed: p = P(χ²df ≥ χ²obs). It is computed here from the regularized incomplete gamma function (numerical methods like those of Homework 6): p = Q(df/2, χ²/2). Since χ² grows with n, the effect size Cramér's V = √(χ² / (n·min(r−1, c−1))) ∈ [0, 1] is also reported. Each cell's Pearson residual (O − E)/√E shows where the deviation comes from (|residual| ≥ 2 is notable).

5. Interpretation

Statistical association is not causality

  • The χ² test only says whether the data are compatible with independence. It is symmetric in X and F and gives no direction: "X causes F", "F causes X" and "a third factor causes both" give the same table.
  • Confounding: a hidden variable Z that influences both X and F creates an association with no causal link (and can even reverse it: Simpson's paradox).
  • Causal conclusions need more than a significant test: a randomized experiment (assignment to X independent of everything else) or explicit causal assumptions.
  • A small p-value is not a large effect (with large n tiny deviations are "significant": look at Cramér's V), and a large p-value does not prove independence: it may only mean the sample is too small to detect the association.

What would we see if H₀ were true?

The simulation draws 2000 datasets of the same size with X and F generated independently, using the observed marginal frequencies, and computes χ² on each. About α of them should be (wrongly) significant, and the average χ² should be close to df.
