Part 1: Modes of Convergence in Probability Theory

1.1 The four modes

A random variable is a function of the outcome ω of an experiment, so a sequence X₁, X₂, … of random variables is a sequence of functions, and "Xn converges to X" can be understood in several non-equivalent ways. Think of Xn as the result of repeating a measurement with n observations.

ModeDefinitionIntuition
In probability
Xn →p X		for every ε > 0: P(|Xn − X| > ε) → 0For each large n, the chance that Xn is far from X is small. It says nothing about what happens to one given sequence of results as n increases.
Almost surely
Xn →a.s. X		P( lim Xn = X ) = 1; equivalently, for every ε > 0: P( supk ≥ n |Xk − X| > ε ) → 0With probability 1, a single trajectory X₁(ω), X₂(ω), … converges to X(ω) as an ordinary sequence of numbers: after some (random) n it stays close forever.
In mean square (L²)
Xn →L² X		E[ (Xn − X)² ] → 0The average squared error vanishes. It also controls the size of the large errors, not just their probability.
In distribution
Xn →d X		Fn(x) → F(x) at every x where F is continuousOnly the shape of the probability law converges (histograms look more and more alike). The Xn need not be close to X, or even defined on the same experiment.

  • L² ⇒ probability by Chebyshev–Markov: P(|Xn − X| > ε) ≤ E[(Xn − X)²]/ε².
  • a.s. ⇒ probability because the event {|Xn − X| > ε} is contained in {supk≥n |Xk − X| > ε}, whose probability tends to 0.

1.3 Counterexamples (simulated)

The converse implications fail. Three sequences show it (ε is the tolerance used below; the quantities are estimated on 2000 simulated trajectories and the exact value is in brackets):

  • (a) Xk independent, equal to 1 with probability 1/k and 0 otherwise. Then P(Xn = 1) = 1/n → 0 and E[Xn²] = 1/n → 0, so Xn → 0 in probability and in L². But Σ1/k diverges, so by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma Xk = 1 infinitely often with probability 1: no almost sure convergence. Observe that the chance of seeing at least one "1" between n and 10n is 1 − n/(10n) = 0.9 for every n.
  • (b) U uniform on (0,1), Xk = k if U < 1/k, else 0. For a given U, Xk = 0 for all k > 1/U: it converges to 0 almost surely (hence in probability), but E[Xn²] = n² · (1/n) = n → ∞: no L² convergence. (Rare but huge values.)
  • (c) X standard normal and Xn = −X for all n. Xn has exactly the law of X, so Xn → X in distribution, but |Xn − X| = 2|X| never gets small: no convergence in probability.

"Sup" column: probability that |Xk − limit| exceeds ε for at least one k in (n, 10n] (a finite-horizon version of the a.s. criterion; the value does not go to 0 in (a) and (c), and does in (b)).

1.4 Role in the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem

Let X₁, X₂, … be i.i.d. with mean μ and variance σ², Sn = X₁ + … + Xn and X̄n = Sn/n.

ResultStatementModeComment
Weak LLNX̄n → μin probabilityWith finite variance it follows from Chebyshev; in fact E[(X̄n − μ)²] = σ²/n → 0, so also in L² (the quantity measured in Homework 4).

1.5 Other important roles in statistics

  • Consistency of estimators: θ̂n → θ in probability (weak) or a.s. (strong). For L², the mean squared error MSE = Var(θ̂) + bias² tending to 0 is a sufficient condition (for X̄n, MSE = σ²/n; for the sample variance s², unbiased, MSE → 0 as well).
  • Slutsky's theorem: if Xn →d X and Yn →p c, then Xn + Yn →d X + c and XnYn →d cX. This is why in the CLT one may replace the unknown σ by the sample standard deviation s (t statistic) and still get N(0, 1).
  • Delta method and asymptotic normality: from √n(θ̂ − θ) →d N(0, v) one gets approximate confidence intervals for θ and for smooth functions g(θ); maximum-likelihood estimators are consistent and asymptotically normal.
  • Glivenko–Cantelli: the empirical distribution function converges a.s. and uniformly to the true F, the basis of the use of histograms and of the bootstrap. Monte Carlo: an integral estimated by an average converges by the LLN with error of order 1/√N.

1.6 Why does √n appear?

1. Additivity of variance. In general Var(Sn) = Σ Var(Xi) + 2 Σi<j Cov(Xi, Xj). If the Xi are independent (uncorrelated is enough) all covariances vanish and Var(Sn) = nσ². Variances add, standard deviations do not: sd(Sn) = σ√n and sd(X̄n) = σ/√n. The "random errors partially cancel" effect is exactly why the spread grows as √n rather than n.

2. The only scaling with a non-degenerate limit. Divide the centred sum by nα: its variance is σ²n1−2α. For α < ½ it explodes, for α > ½ it collapses to 0 (this is the LLN: Sn/n → 0 after centring), and only α = ½ keeps variance σ² for all n. The CLT says that, with this scaling, the whole shape converges to a normal law.

3. Statistical inference. The standard error of the mean is σ/√n, confidence intervals are x̄ ± z·σ/√n, the t statistic is √n (x̄ − μ₀)/s, and estimators are "√n-consistent". Practical consequence: precision improves slowly, with four times the data needed to halve the error. The same 1/√n scaling appears in the Monte Carlo error and in the width of Bayesian posteriors (Part 2).

4. The role of independence. If the Xi are positively correlated (for example an AR(1) series with correlation ρ), Var(Sn) ≈ nσ²(1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ): still of order n, but with a larger constant (equivalently, a smaller effective sample size). With perfect dependence (all Xi equal) Sn = nX₁, sd(Sn) = σn and not even the LLN holds. If the variance is infinite (e.g. the Cauchy distribution) the scaling √n and the CLT fail: the sample mean of Cauchy variables has the same distribution as a single observation.

For independent N(0,1) data the standard deviation of Sn/√n stays at σ = 1 (it does not depend on n), while that of the mean Sn/n falls like 1/√n. With correlation ρ the limit is √((1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ)); with perfect correlation it grows like √n.

Part 2: Conditional Probability, Bayes' Theorem and Statistical Inference

2.1 Conditional probability

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B), for P(B) > 0.

In probability theory it is the probability of A when the sample space is restricted to the outcomes in which B occurred. It gives the multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) P(B) and the law of total probability P(B) = Σ P(B | Ai) P(Ai) for a partition A₁, …, Ak. A and B are independent exactly when P(A | B) = P(A). In statistics the same idea appears as conditional relative frequencies in a contingency table (Homework 7), conditional distributions and regression functions E[Y | X = x]. Note that in general P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A): confusing the two is the root of many errors (base-rate fallacy, "prosecutor's fallacy").

2.2 Bayes' theorem and its three components

P(A | B) = P(B | A) P(A) / P(B), with P(B) = Σj P(B | Aj) P(Aj).

In inference the unknown is a parameter θ and the evidence is the data x:

π(θ | x) = L(θ; x) · π(θ) / ∫ L(θ; x) π(θ) dθ  ∝  L(θ; x) · π(θ)
  • Prior π(θ): what we believe about θ before seeing the data (previous studies, expert knowledge, or a deliberately vague choice).
  • Likelihood L(θ; x) = p(x | θ): the probability (density) of the observed data for each value of θ, viewed as a function of θ. It is not a probability distribution over θ (it does not integrate to 1 in θ); it measures how well each θ explains the data.
  • Posterior π(θ | x): the updated belief about θ after the data. It is a genuine probability distribution for the parameter, so we can say "θ lies in this interval with probability 95%" (credible interval).
  • Evidence (denominator): a normalising constant; it also allows the comparison of models.

Why updating is central. Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior combines information sources in a coherent way. The posterior can be used as the prior for new data (sequential updating; for i.i.d. data the order of the observations does not matter), it gives direct probability statements about parameters and hypotheses (the frequentist p-value instead gives P(data | H₀), not P(H₀ | data)), it works with small samples, and it provides predictions that account for the uncertainty about θ.

2.3 Example 1: an intrusion detection system and the base rate

An IDS raises an alert with probability sensitivity = P(alert | attack) and a false alert with probability false positive rate = P(alert | no attack). The prior is the base rate P(attack) of attacks among the events observed. Bayes gives the posterior P(attack | alert).


            

            
With a very good detector (99% sensitivity, 1% false alarms) but rare attacks (0.1%), only about 9% of the alerts are real attacks: the likelihood is high but the small prior dominates. The conclusion changes completely with the prior, which is why ignoring the base rate is a serious error.

2.5 Advantages, limitations and influence on conclusions

            
    
                
  • Advantages: uses prior information (previous studies, experts, regulations); direct probabilistic statements about parameters and hypotheses; natural for sequential problems and small samples; handles hierarchical and complex models and produces predictive distributions; a single coherent rule (Bayes) for all inference.
    • 
                
  • Limitations: the prior is a modelling choice and can be subjective; with little data it can dominate the posterior (see the "strong and wrong" prior: with n = 10 the posterior is still pulled towards the prior, and it needs many observations to be corrected); "non-informative" priors are not unique and not invariant under reparametrisation (uniform in p is not uniform in the odds); a wrong likelihood (model) gives wrong conclusions however much data we have; the denominator is an integral that is only explicit for conjugate models, otherwise it requires numerical methods (MCMC, variational inference).
    • 
                
  • Practice: report the prior, check the sensitivity of the conclusions to reasonable alternative priors (as in the table above); when there is a lot of data all reasonable priors lead to the same conclusion (and the Bayesian and frequentist answers agree), while with little data the prior matters and has to be stated openly.

2.6 Summary

            
Conditional probability is the tool; Bayes' theorem turns P(data | θ) into P(θ | data). Prior, likelihood and posterior are the starting belief, the evidence and the updated belief. The posterior is the product of the first two (up to normalisation), and as n grows the likelihood dominates and the posterior concentrates at rate 1/√n, linking this part to the asymptotic results of Part 1.
