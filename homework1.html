3. Cryptographic Properties & Discussion

            
    
                
  • Uniformity and State-Space Limitations: Although local changes in $k$ yield coordinate shifts that do not follow a simple linear equation ($y = mx + q$), the outputs are strictly bounded within $\mathbb{Z}_{17}$ and exhibit a repeating cyclic order. This proves that the toy model fails to provide secure cryptographic unpredictability, not because of its deterministic nature—since all one-way cryptographic functions and key derivation functions are strictly deterministic—but rather due to the minuscule size of its state group order (the curve group has only 18 points and G generates a subgroup of order 9).
    • 
                
  • Toy vs Real Elliptic Curve: As the Coordinate Distribution Table shows, the scalar multiplication sequence wraps around into a small cyclic group after a few iterations, causing points to repeat. This results in multiple private keys generating the same public point (collision). In real-world elliptic curves, like Ethereum's secp256k1, the scale of the group order is exponentially higher ($\approx 2^{256}$), making collisions or accidental key overlaps statistically impossible.
    • 
                
  • Avalanche Effect: Changing $k$ from 6 to 7 completely alters the binary representation of the resulting coordinates. The avalanche effect is a fundamental property of cryptographic hash functions and block ciphers, ensuring high diffusion where a minor input modification radically randomizes the output. In algebraic group structures like elliptic curves, this non-linearity ensures that small variations in the scalar private key yield completely decorrelated public points.

There is no visible geometric pattern between consecutive points: the multiples of G jump around the grid. This is the visual intuition behind the hardness of the discrete logarithm problem. Here the sequence closes after only a few steps (the order of G is 9), so the problem is trivial.
