Homework 3: Cryptographic Schemes & Statistical Cryptanalysis

Comparative analysis between Toy ECC (secp256k1), Caesar Cipher, and Cryptanalytic Attack Vectors

1. Toy ECC Scheme vs. Caesar Cipher & Message Encryption

Building upon the toy elliptic curve developed in Part 1 ($y^2 = x^3 + 7 \pmod p$), we analyze message encryption and decryption procedures across asymmetric and symmetric primitives using a concrete sample message.

Concrete Example: Encrypting the Message "CYBER"

A. The Caesar Cipher Implementation

  • Key ($k$): Shift of $3$ positions.
  • Plaintext: C Y B E R $\rightarrow$ Numeric values: `[2, 24, 1, 4, 17]`
  • Encryption ($C = P + 3 \pmod{26}$): [5, 1, 4, 7, 20] $\rightarrow$ Ciphertext: F B E H U
  • Decryption ($P = C - 3 \pmod{26}$): Recovers successfully the numeric array [2, 24, 1, 4, 17] $\rightarrow$ C Y B E R

B. Toy ECC Scheme Implementation (EC-ElGamal style)

  • Parameters: Base point $G$, Private key $d$ (restricted scalar), Public key $Q = d \cdot G$.
  • Message Mapping: Map a character or block index to a valid point on the curve, e.g., $M = (x_m, y_m)$.
  • Encryption: Choose a random ephemeral scalar $r$. Compute the ciphertext pair $(C_1, C_2)$: $$C_1 = r \cdot G$$ $$C_2 = M + r \cdot Q$$
  • Decryption: Recover the original message point by subtracting the secret multiple of $C_1$: $$M = C_2 - d \cdot C_1$$ *(Since $d \cdot C_1 = d(r \cdot G) = r(d \cdot G) = r \cdot Q$, the masking term $r \cdot Q$ is completely canceled out).*

Comparative Summary

Feature Caesar Cipher Toy ECC Scheme
Type Symmetric (Monoalphabetic Substitution) Asymmetric (Public-Key Cryptography)
Key Space Tiny ($26$ possible shifts) Restricted in toy model (e.g., $2^{20}$), large in standard ($2^{256}$)
Statistical Property Preserves plaintext letter frequencies (vulnerable) Provides high entropy and diffusion
Mathematical Basis Modular arithmetic ($\mathbb{Z}_{26}$) Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm Problem (ECDLP)

2. Statistical Cryptanalysis of the Caesar Cipher

Because the Caesar cipher does not mask language redundancy, it is vulnerable to statistical attacks without knowing the key.

Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) Scoring Algorithm:
1. Count letter frequencies $O_i$ in the candidate plaintext.
2. Compare them against expected English letter frequencies $E_i$.
3. Compute $\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}$.
4. The correct key is the shift that minimizes the $\chi^2$ statistic.

Effectiveness & Limitations

  • Large Ciphertexts ($\ge 500$ chars): Extremely effective; empirical frequencies closely match theoretical expectations.
  • Short Ciphertexts ($\le 20$ chars): Fails reliably due to high statistical variance and noise (missing common letters like 'e' or 't'), requiring n-gram scoring frameworks instead.

3. Attack on the Toy ECC Scheme & Security Scaling

Restricting the private key space of the secp256k1-derived toy curve to a small range (e.g., $2^{20}$) makes the scheme computationally insecure.

  • The Attack: Utilizing optimized brute-force or Baby-Step Giant-Step (BSGS) algorithms, an attacker can recover the private key $d$ from $Q = d \cdot G$ in $O(\sqrt{N})$ group operations—taking milliseconds for $2^{20}$.
  • Difference vs. Caesar Attack: The Caesar attack exploits linguistic and statistical redundancy of human text. The Toy ECC attack exploits an architectural/parameter vulnerability (insufficient entropy and restricted key space), while the underlying algebraic hardness of the curve remains sound.
  • Why Real ECC is Secure: Standard secp256k1 operates over an order $n \approx 2^{256}$. General ECDLP algorithms like Pollard's Rho require $\approx 2^{128}$ operations, making computational inversion physically impossible.
