2. Statistical Cryptanalysis of the Caesar Cipher

Because substitution ciphers do not alter the underlying statistical frequencies of the natural language, we can design an automated cryptanalytic algorithm to recover the hidden key without prior knowledge.

Statistical Frequency Analysis Engine

The automated script executes the following cryptanalytic pipeline:

  1. Letter Frequency Profiling: Counts occurrences of characters in the ciphertext and computes normalized frequency percentages.
  2. Language Vector Comparison: Evaluates chi-squared ($\chi^2$) statistics or absolute deviations against standard English/Italian letter distributions (where E, A, and I dominate).
  3. Key Recovery: Identifies the optimal rotational offset minimizing statistical distance, successfully decrypting the ciphertext.

Effectiveness, Text Length Dependency, and Limitations

  • Long Ciphertexts: As text size increases ($N > 200$ characters), empirical letter frequencies converge toward theoretical linguistic expectations. The attack succeeds with near 100% reliability and negligible computational cost.
  • Short Ciphertexts: For brief snippets ($N < 20$ characters), statistical variance is too high. Common letters like 'E' might be entirely absent or overrepresented by chance (e.g., a sentence dominated by 'O'), leading to false key estimates or complete failure of frequency-based cryptanalysis.
  • Alternative Statistical Approaches: While monoalphabetic frequency counts suffice for Caesar, advanced classical ciphers require n-gram scoring (digrams/trigrams) and index of coincidence calculations to break polyalphabetic structures.

Critical Comparison: Toy ECC Exploit vs. Caesar Statistical Attack

  • Nature of the Vulnerability: The Caesar attack exploits linguistic redundancy and statistical leakage. Conversely, the Toy ECC attack exploits an artificial parameter constraint (an intentionally minuscule key space).
  • Why Real-World ECC Resists Brute-Force: A production-grade Ethereum/Bitcoin wallet utilizing standard **secp256k1** operates over a private key space of $2^{256}$ ($\approx 1.15 \times 10^{77}$ possible keys). Even if an attacker harnessed all global computational power for millennia, exhaustive search remains physically impossible. Furthermore, modern ECC outputs exhibit zero statistical correlation with input keys, neutralizing frequency analysis entirely.
