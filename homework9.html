1. Correlation

Given paired observations (x₁, y₁), …, (xn, yn), write x̄ and ȳ for the means and

Sxx = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²,   Syy = Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)²,   Sxy = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) = Σxᵢyᵢ − n·x̄·ȳ
sample covariance sxy = Sxy/(n − 1), variances sx² = Sxx/(n − 1), sy² = Syy/(n − 1)
Pearson correlation coefficient: r = sxy / (sx sy) = Sxy / √(Sxx Syy)

For random variables the same quantities are Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μX)(Y − μY)] = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y] and ρ = Cov(X, Y)/(σX σY). The covariance says whether the two variables tend to be above/below their means together (positive) or in opposite ways (negative), but its size depends on the units; dividing by the standard deviations gives a unit-free number.

Properties

  • −1 ≤ r ≤ 1 (proved in section 2), with |r| = 1 exactly when all the points lie on a straight line (r = +1 increasing, −1 decreasing).
  • Invariance: replacing X by aX + b and Y by cY + d (a, c > 0) does not change r; if a·c < 0 only the sign changes. So r does not depend on units or origin.
  • It measures only linear association. r ≈ 0 does not mean "no relationship": a perfect parabola can have r = 0 (see section 4 and the datasets below). Anscombe's four datasets have the same r ≈ 0.816 and yet look completely different.
  • Sensitive to outliers and to restricted ranges of the data.
  • Correlation is not causation: as in Homework 7, an association can come from a common cause, reverse causation or chance.

2. Regression: Solving for a and b

Problem. Find the line ŷ = a + b·x that best predicts y from x, in the sense of least squares: minimise the sum of the squared vertical errors

S(a, b) = Σ (yᵢ − a − b·xᵢ)²

Solution by calculus (normal equations)

∂S/∂a = −2 Σ (yᵢ − a − b xᵢ) = 0  ⇒  Σyᵢ = n·a + b·Σxᵢ  ⇒  a = ȳ − b·x̄
∂S/∂b = −2 Σ xᵢ(yᵢ − a − b xᵢ) = 0  ⇒  Σxᵢyᵢ = a·Σxᵢ + b·Σxᵢ²
Substituting a = ȳ − b x̄ in the second equation: Σxᵢyᵢ − n x̄ ȳ = b (Σxᵢ² − n x̄²), that is b = Sxy / Sxx = sxy / sx².

It is a minimum: the Hessian of S is [[2n, 2Σx], [2Σx, 2Σx²]], with determinant 4(nΣx² − (Σx)²) = 4n·Sxx > 0 as soon as the xᵢ are not all equal.

Alternative derivation without derivatives

Put uᵢ = xᵢ − x̄ and vᵢ = yᵢ − ȳ (so Σu = Σv = 0). Then yᵢ − a − bxᵢ = (vᵢ − b uᵢ) + (ȳ − a − b x̄), and the cross term vanishes, so

S(a, b) = Σ (vᵢ − b uᵢ)² + n (ȳ − a − b x̄)² = Syy − 2b Sxy + b² Sxx + n (ȳ − a − b x̄)²

The second term is zero for a = ȳ − b x̄; the first is a parabola in b with minimum at b = Sxy/Sxx. The line always passes through the point of means (x̄, ȳ).

The two regression lines

The roles of x and y are not symmetric. Minimising the vertical errors gives the regression of Y on X; minimising the horizontal errors gives the regression of X on Y:

Y on XX on Y
Lineŷ = ayx + byx·xx̂ = axy + bxy·y
Slopebyx = Sxy/Sxx = r·sy/sxbxy = Sxy/Syy = r·sx/sy
Interceptayx = ȳ − byx x̄axy = x̄ − bxy ȳ

Both lines pass through (x̄, ȳ). Drawn in the same (x, y) plane, the second one has slope 1/bxy = Syy/Sxy, which is steeper than byx unless |r| = 1: the two lines coincide only for a perfect linear relation, and they are perpendicular (horizontal and vertical) when r = 0.

Relation between r and the regression coefficients

byx · bxy = (Sxy/Sxx)(Sxy/Syy) = Sxy² / (Sxx Syy) = r²
Both slopes have the sign of Sxy, which is the sign of r. Hence r = ± √(byx · bxy), i.e. |r| is the geometric mean of the two regression coefficients and r has their common sign.
  • Why |r| ≤ 1. At the optimum, SSE = Syy − byx² Sxx = Syy − Sxy²/Sxx = Syy(1 − r²). Since SSE ≥ 0, r² ≤ 1; and r² = 1 exactly when SSE = 0 (all points on the line).
  • Coefficient of determination: R² = 1 − SSE/SST = r² is the fraction of the variance of y explained by the linear dependence on x.
  • Regression to the mean: in standardised variables the line is ẑy = r·zx. Since |r| < 1, an individual 2 standard deviations above the mean in x is predicted to be only 2r above the mean in y.

4. Independence Implies Uncorrelation

Theorem. If X and Y are independent random variables with finite variances (and σX, σY > 0), then Cov(X, Y) = 0, hence ρ(X, Y) = 0.

Proof. Since Cov(X, Y) = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y], it is enough to show E[XY] = E[X]E[Y].

  • Discrete case. Independence means P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x)·P(Y = y) for all x, y. Then E[XY] = Σx Σy x·y·P(X = x, Y = y) = Σx Σy x·y·P(X = x)P(Y = y) = (Σx x P(X = x)) (Σy y P(Y = y)) = E[X]E[Y].
  • Continuous case. Independence means that the joint density factorises, f(x, y) = fX(x) fY(y). Then E[XY] = ∬ x y fX(x) fY(y) dx dy = (∫ x fX(x) dx)(∫ y fY(y) dy) = E[X]E[Y] (Fubini's theorem applies because E|XY| = E|X|·E|Y| < ∞, finite since the variances are finite.)
  • General case. Independence means that the joint law is the product of the marginal laws; the same factorisation follows from Fubini–Tonelli.

Therefore Cov(X, Y) = E[X]E[Y] − E[X]E[Y] = 0 and ρ = 0. ∎

Remark. Independence is much stronger: it gives E[g(X)h(Y)] = E[g(X)]E[h(Y)] for all (bounded) functions g, h, while uncorrelation is the same statement only for g(x) = x and h(y) = y, i.e. it rules out only linear association.

The converse is false

Let X be N(0, 1) and Y = X². Then Y is completely determined by X, so they are not independent (for instance P(Y > 1 | |X| > 1) = 1, while P(Y > 1) ≈ 0.317). Yet E[X] = 0 and E[XY] = E[X³] = 0 (odd moments of a symmetric law vanish), so Cov(X, Y) = 0. The same happens for the "perfect parabola" dataset above (r = 0 although y = x²). The converse is true in one important case: for a jointly normal pair, uncorrelated ⇔ independent (with ρ = 0 the joint density factorises).

Simulation

For independent N(0, 1) samples of size n the sample correlation r is not exactly 0, but fluctuates around 0 with standard deviation 1/√(n − 1) (the √n law again). For the pair (X, X²) the correlation is also near 0, yet the variables are strongly dependent: the correlation between |X| and Y = X² is about 0.94 (theory), which the linear coefficient r(X, Y) completely misses.

5. Conclusions

  • Correlation measures the strength and direction of the linear association; regression turns it into a prediction rule. The slope of each regression line is r times a ratio of standard deviations, and r² is the share of variance explained.
  • There are two regression lines. They meet at the point of means and coincide only when |r| = 1. The product of the two slopes is r², so |r| is their geometric mean, as verified numerically above.
  • Independence ⇒ uncorrelation, but uncorrelation does not imply independence. Zero correlation excludes only linear dependence; a plot of the data should always accompany r (Anscombe).
  • A strong correlation, even a significant one, is not evidence of causation.
