Exercise 1: Welford's Recurrence Formulas

Starting point: Knuth's recurrence for the mean

Let Mk = (x₁ + … + xk)/k be the mean of the first k values. Since k·Mk = (k−1)·Mk−1 + xk, we get

Mk = Mk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)/k

The new mean is the old one plus a fraction 1/k of the "surprise" δ = xk − Mk−1. No past values need to be stored.

Same approach for the sum of squares

Define the sum of squares of the deviations SSk = Σi≤k (xi − Mk)². We want SSk in terms of SSk−1. Put δ = xk − Mk−1. From Knuth's formula:

  • Mk = Mk−1 + δ/k
  • xk − Mk = δ − δ/k = δ(k−1)/k

For each of the first k−1 values, write xi − Mk = (xi − Mk−1) − δ/k and expand the square:

Σi<k (xi − Mk)² = SSk−1 − 2(δ/k)·Σi<k(xi − Mk−1) + (k−1)δ²/k²
The middle sum is 0 (deviations from the mean always add up to zero), so this equals SSk−1 + (k−1)δ²/k².

Adding the last term (xk − Mk)² = δ²(k−1)²/k²:

SSk = SSk−1 + δ²(k−1)/k² + δ²(k−1)²/k² = SSk−1 + δ²(k−1)/k

Finally, since xk − Mk−1 = δ and xk − Mk = δ(k−1)/k, their product is exactly δ²(k−1)/k. Therefore Welford's formula:

SSk = SSk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)(xk − Mk)
and the sample variance is s²k = SSk/(k−1) (for k ≥ 2); the population-style variance is SSk/k.

The code

let mean = 0, SS = 0;                 // initial values
for (let k = 1; k <= n; k++) {
    const x = data[k - 1];
    const delta = x - mean;           // 1) surprise
    mean += delta / k;                // 2) Knuth's recurrence for the mean
    SS += delta * (x - mean);         // 3) Welford's recurrence for the sum of squares
}
const variance = SS / (n - 1);        // sample variance

Why not use the textbook formula?

            
The one-pass formula (Σx² − n·mean²)/(n − 1) subtracts two huge, almost equal numbers when the data are large compared with their spread, and loses all precision. Welford only works with the small deviations. Test data: 10⁹ + {4, 7, 13, 16}, whose exact sample variance is 30.

            

        

 
        
        

            
Exercise 2: Sampling Mean and Variance

            
Setup. Each penetration attempt gets an integer score from 0 to 10, drawn from a fixed distribution with theoretical mean μ and variance σ². There are m hackers; each attempts n systems and so gets a trajectory of n scores. For each hacker, Welford's formulas give the sampling mean x̄ and the sampling variance s² = SS/(n−1). The page then averages these m values and compares them with μ and σ². It also shows the biased version SS/n to explain why n−1 is used.

Conclusions

            
    
                
  • Unbiasedness. The average of the m sampling means is very close to μ, and the average of the sampling variances (÷(n−1)) is very close to σ² for every n, even n = 2. Formally E[x̄] = μ and E[s²] = σ²: on average the estimators are right, so they have no systematic error.
    • 
                
  • Why n−1. The deviations are measured from x̄, not from μ, and the data are always closer to their own mean than to μ: Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² = Σ(xᵢ − μ)² − n(x̄ − μ)². Dividing by n therefore underestimates σ² by exactly the factor (n−1)/n, as the table shows (the biased column matches σ²(n−1)/n); dividing by n−1 (Bessel's correction) removes this bias. The bias matters mostly for small n.
    • 
                
  • Variability. A single hacker's x̄ and s² are random: they change from hacker to hacker. The standard deviation of x̄ across hackers matches σ/√n, and that of s² also shrinks roughly like 1/√n. With larger n the estimates concentrate around the true values (consistency); with small n they scatter widely, even if their average is right.
    • 
                
  • Effect of m. m does not change the distribution of x̄ or s²; it only makes the average over hackers more precise (error about 1/√m). The experiment needs a large m to see the unbiasedness clearly; a single hacker cannot.
    • 
                
  • Why this matters in inferential statistics. In practice we see only one sample and do not know μ and σ². x̄ and s² are the standard estimators of these unknown parameters, and these results justify using them: they are correct on average and get more accurate as n grows. The known spread σ/√n is the standard error, the basis of confidence intervals and hypothesis tests (such as the t-test), and s² is plugged in when σ² is unknown.
    • 
                
  • Why Welford. It computes both quantities in a single pass, with O(1) memory, and is numerically stable: ideal for streams of data (such as attack logs) that cannot be stored.
